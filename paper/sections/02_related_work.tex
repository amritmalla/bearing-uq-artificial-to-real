\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Benchmarks and evaluation of bearing fault diagnosis}

Data-driven bearing fault diagnosis is dominated by a few public benchmarks, above all the Case Western Reserve
University (CWRU) data. Using established diagnostic techniques, \citet{smith2015} showed that the CWRU recordings
differ widely in how clearly their faults can be diagnosed, a warning against treating benchmark accuracy as a
measure of real diagnostic ability. A recurring problem is the way data are split. When windows cut from the same
recording, or the same physical bearing, appear in both training and test sets, models can recognise the bearing
instead of its damage, and accuracy is inflated. \citet{vieira2026} demonstrate this across four datasets, including
Paderborn, and recommend bearing-wise splits, noting that the number of distinct training bearings decides how well
models generalise. \citet{gonzalezgarcia2026} likewise evaluate at the recording level and find F1 scores far below
those reported with random window splits. We follow this line and split strictly by bearing.

\subsection{Artificial and real damage}

Faults for laboratory experiments are usually made artificially, because natural damage takes a long time to develop. The
Paderborn dataset \citep{lessmeier2016} was designed to expose the resulting gap: it contains bearings with damage
introduced by drilling, electric discharge machining and electric engraving, and bearings whose damage developed in
accelerated lifetime tests. Its authors reported that training on artificial and on real damage leads to different
classification accuracies. \citet{hassannejad2025} trained physics-informed convolutional networks on the
Paderborn vibration signals separately for each damage type and reached 83--90\,\% test accuracy on artificial and
96\,\% on real damage. Their recordings were split 75/12.5/12.5 into training, validation and test sets within each
damage type, without a stated grouping by bearing, so the results describe neither transfer from artificial to real
damage nor, demonstrably, generalisation to unseen bearings; the much lower accuracies we report on real damage
(Section~\ref{sec:results}) answer a different question. Much of the work on domain shift in fault diagnosis
concerns changing operating conditions---speed, load or radial force---and aims to recover accuracy through domain
adaptation, for example by re-estimating batch-normalisation statistics \citep{li2018,zhang2017}, by adversarial
training \citep{ganin2016} or by matching feature statistics \citep{sun2016}. These studies mostly measure success by
accuracy; whether a model's confidence remains trustworthy after the shift has received far less attention.

\subsection{Calibration and uncertainty under distribution shift}

Modern neural networks are often miscalibrated, and a single temperature parameter fitted on held-out data can correct
much of this in-distribution \citep{guo2017}. Other post-hoc methods include Platt scaling \citep{platt1999} and
isotonic regression \citep{zadrozny2002}. Under distribution shift, however, calibration obtained on the source
distribution tends to degrade: in a large comparison of uncertainty methods, \citet{ovadia2019} found that the quality
of predictive uncertainty deteriorates as the shift grows. In fault diagnosis, \citet{forest2024} make calibration
part of domain adaptation and report lower calibration error across operating-condition shifts on the Paderborn
dataset, using motor-current signals and unlabelled target data. Closest to our work, \citet{gonzalezgarcia2026}
evaluate calibration and conformal prediction for vibration-based bearing classifiers on CWRU and the Society for Machinery Failure Prevention Technology (MFPT) data. They find that
raw probabilities are well calibrated but that Platt scaling degrades under a shift in fault severity, and that split
conformal prediction loses its coverage guarantee when class proportions differ between calibration and test
recordings, which class-conditional conformal prediction partly repairs.

\subsection{Conformal prediction and selective classification}

Split conformal prediction turns any classifier into one that outputs prediction sets with a finite-sample coverage
guarantee, provided calibration and test data are exchangeable \citep{vovk2005,angelopoulos2023}. Class-conditional
(Mondrian) variants guarantee coverage per class \citep{vovk2005}, and adaptive scores produce sets that better
reflect per-example difficulty \citep{romano2020}. Under covariate shift, coverage can be restored by weighting
calibration scores with the likelihood ratio between test and calibration inputs \citep{tibshirani2019}---which
requires that ratio to be estimated, typically from unlabelled target data. Selective classification lets a model
abstain on uncertain inputs, trading coverage (the share of inputs decided automatically) against risk (the error
among them), summarised by the risk--coverage curve \citep{geifman2017}.

\paragraph{Gap addressed.} To our knowledge, no study has examined whether calibrated confidence and the resulting
automation decisions in vibration-based bearing diagnosis survive the shift from artificial to real damage, with
splits by bearing and uncertainty estimated over bearings. This is the question we study.
