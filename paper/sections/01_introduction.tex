\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Rolling-element bearings are among the most common components of rotating machinery and a frequent cause of its
failure. Vibration-based condition monitoring detects bearing damage from the periodic impacts it produces, and
machine-learning classifiers are increasingly used to automate the diagnosis. In practice, automation rarely means
replacing the maintenance engineer. A realistic system decides on its own when it is confident and refers the
remaining cases to a person. Such a system is only as safe as its confidence: a model that is confidently wrong will
act on its mistakes without asking anyone.

Confidence is therefore a quantity that has to be trustworthy in its own right. A model is \emph{calibrated} when its
stated confidence matches how often it is correct; a threshold on calibrated confidence can then be chosen to keep
the error among automated decisions below a target. Conformal prediction offers a stronger, distribution-free
guarantee: prediction sets that contain the true class with a chosen probability. Both rest on an assumption that is
easy to overlook---that the data used to set thresholds resemble the data the system will meet in operation.

For bearing diagnosis this assumption is under particular strain. Classifiers are typically developed on faults that
were made artificially, by drilling, electric discharge machining or engraving, because natural damage takes a long
time to develop. In service, they must diagnose damage that grew through fatigue and wear. The Paderborn bearing
dataset \citep{lessmeier2016} contains both kinds and shows that the difference matters for accuracy. Much less is
known about what it does to confidence. If a model's calibration, its automation thresholds and its conformal
guarantees are all established on artificial damage, do they still hold on real damage? And if not, how much labelled
real data is needed to restore them?

We study these questions with three standard classifiers---Random Forest, support vector machine and XGBoost---on
envelope-spectrum features of the Paderborn vibration signals. Models are trained and calibrated on artificially
damaged bearings and tested on bearings with real damage, with every bearing assigned to exactly one split. Because
results depend strongly on which bearings are held out for calibration, we repeat the experiment for all 105
possible choices and estimate uncertainty with a bootstrap over bearings. The main analysis uses one operating
condition; we repeat it at the dataset's three other conditions and with an adaptive demodulation band. We ask:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*}]
  \item How much do accuracy and calibration degrade from artificial to real damage?
  \item Do confidence thresholds, post-hoc calibration and conformal prediction fitted on artificial damage still
        hold on real damage?
  \item How many labelled real bearings are needed to restore trustworthy confidence?
\end{enumerate}

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item An evaluation of calibration and selective automation under the artificial-to-real damage shift, with
        bearing-level splits, 105 calibration-bearing rotations and bootstrap intervals over bearings.
  \item Evidence that confidence thresholds chosen on artificial damage for a 5\,\% error target produce 24--27\,\%
        error on real damage, exceeding the target in every rotation, and that split conformal coverage falls to
        0.65--0.68 against a nominal 0.90---although the same models are close to calibrated on unseen artificial
        bearings.
  \item The identification of ``silent'' real-damage bearings, whose fault signature is indistinguishable from a
        healthy bearing's in the envelope spectrum and which are therefore confidently diagnosed as healthy or as
        the wrong fault; two of them are misclassified at every operating condition of the dataset.
  \item A repetition at four operating conditions, showing that the failure is strongest where the features
        separate artificial damage best, and that missed faults dominate the errors only at those conditions.
  \item A test of calibrating on one or two labelled real bearings per class, which lowers the error at the 5\,\%
        target to 0.10--0.11 but does not reliably meet it, and makes the system automate fewer cases.
  \item A feature ablation showing that amplitude-based time-domain features describe individual bearings rather
        than their damage, and hide the artificial-to-real gap behind a poor in-domain reference.
\end{itemize}
