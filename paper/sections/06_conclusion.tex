\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Bearing fault classifiers developed on artificial damage can look trustworthy where they are tested and still fail
where they are used. On the Paderborn dataset, three standard classifiers were close to calibrated on unseen
artificially damaged bearings, yet on real damage their confidence thresholds, post-hoc calibration and conformal
guarantees all failed. A threshold chosen for 5\,\% error gave 24--27\,\% error, in every one of 105 calibration
choices, and conformal coverage fell to 0.65--0.68 against a nominal 0.90. The failures concentrate on real-damage
bearings whose envelope spectra show no fault signature; the models confidently call them healthy, so most errors are
missed faults. Calibrating on one or two labelled real bearings per class reduced the error substantially but did not
reliably reach the target, and the system automated correspondingly fewer cases.

For practice, the lesson is that automation thresholds must be set on data that resemble the damage the system will
meet, that results should be reported per bearing, and that self-normalised fault-frequency features transfer between
bearings better than absolute vibration statistics. Extending the evaluation to other operating conditions, adaptive
demodulation bands and field data would show how general these effects are.
