\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Bearing fault classifiers developed on artificial damage can look trustworthy where they are tested and still fail
where they are used. On the Paderborn dataset, three standard feature-based classifiers were close to calibrated on unseen
artificially damaged bearings, yet on real damage their confidence thresholds, post-hoc calibration and conformal
guarantees all failed. At the main operating condition, a threshold chosen for 5\,\% error gave 24--27\,\% error and
exceeded the target in every one of the 105 calibration choices, and conformal coverage fell to 0.65--0.68 against a nominal 0.90. The failures
concentrate on real-damage bearings whose envelope spectra show little or no fault signature; the models confidently
call most of them healthy, so most errors are missed faults. The same held at lower load torque. At lower radial force and lower speed
the features were weaker, the models less confident and the failure milder, but the target was still exceeded in
most rotations. Calibrating on one or two labelled real bearings per class reduced the error substantially but did
not reliably reach the target, and the system automated correspondingly fewer cases. A 1D-CNN trained on the raw
signal was no remedy: with few training bearings, it was already confidently wrong on unseen artificial bearings,
and adapting it to unlabelled real-damage data improved its accuracy but not the reliability of its confidence.

In terms of our research questions: accuracy fell from 0.88--0.91 to about 0.69 and calibration error rose from
0.01--0.09 to 0.18--0.25 (uncalibrated probabilities; RQ1); confidence thresholds, post-hoc calibration and conformal prediction fitted on
artificial damage all failed on real damage (RQ2); and two labelled real bearings per class were not enough to restore
trustworthy confidence---they cut the error at the 5\,\% target from about 0.27 to 0.10--0.11 but met the target in
only about half of the draws (RQ3).

For practice, the lesson is that automation thresholds must be set on data that resemble the damage the system will
meet, that results should be reported per bearing, and that features and models should be checked on bearings they
have not seen, since amplitude statistics and a CNN trained on few bearings learned bearing identity rather than
damage. Evaluating other bearing types, test rigs and field data would
show how general these effects are.
